# Calibration — where the "top 10% / 5% / 1%" numbers come from

**Read this first: these percentiles are modelled, not measured.** There is no dataset of
real quant applicants behind this app. Anyone shipping 24 bespoke games and claiming
empirical percentiles on all of them is extrapolating too — the difference is that this
file shows the arithmetic so you can disagree with it.

Every anchor in every game file was **generated by the models described below**, not chosen
by hand. The generators live in the project's scratch scripts; the numbers they produced are
reproduced in §5 so you can check them against the `th:` blocks in `js/games/`.

---

## 1. The scoring formula (item-based games)

```
award = base × quality × speed × streak

quality = 1                     correct
        = partial ∈ [0,1]       estimation games (slider)
        = −penalty              wrong

speed   = 1 + 0.5 · clamp(1 − ms/par, −0.6, +0.6)      →  [0.70, 1.30]
streak  = 1 + 0.06 · min(consecutive correct, 10)      →  [1.00, 1.60]
```

`base`, `par` and `penalty` are per-variant constants, visible in each game file.

The five trading games (07, 08, 10, 12, 24) bypass this and score **cumulative PnL**, so
their anchors come from a Monte-Carlo of the game's own mechanics instead (§5b).

## 2. The applicant model

A candidate is a pair: **N** attempts completed in the window, at accuracy **a**.
Rather than use a closed form for the expected score, the model **simulates 6,000 runs** of
the real scoring rule — including the streak multiplier, which is path-dependent and cannot
be written down in closed form.

Crucially, `N` is derived as

```
N = window / (work + overhead)
```

where `work = par × timeFactor` is the thinking time (this is where skill lives) and
`overhead` is the part of a round nobody can compress: feedback display, the flash window in
Order Book Reader, the pre-changepoint watching time in Regime Shift. Ignoring that overhead
was the single largest error in an earlier version of this table — it inflated the detection
games by up to 8×.

## 3. The (timeFactor, accuracy) assumptions per percentile

| band | timeFactor | reasoning task | arithmetic sprint | binary choice | slider (partial credit) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| p50 | 1.45 × par | 48% | 80% | 55% | 50% pass, 0.45 credit |
| p90 | 0.95 × par | 76% | 93% | 85% | 80% pass, 0.72 credit |
| p95 | 0.85 × par | 83% | 95% | 90% | 88% pass, 0.80 credit |
| p99 | 0.72 × par | 92% | 97% | 96% | 95% pass, 0.90 credit |

Target Sum uses a fifth profile — accuracy is always 100% because wrong answers are
impossible there, so it is a pure throughput test.

The median row is deliberately unflattering. Applicant pools for these roles are
self-selected but large, and the modal candidate can do the maths but not under a
75-second clock — which is exactly what the games measure.

## 4. From four anchors to a percentile

Latent ability is assumed normal. The anchors are pinned to their z-values —
`p50 → 0`, `p90 → 1.2816`, `p95 → 1.6449`, `p99 → 2.3263` — your score is interpolated
piecewise-linearly in z between them, and the tail is read off the normal CDF
(`js/core/percentile.js`). Scores above the top-1% anchor extrapolate along the
p95→p99 slope, so "Top 0.2%" is reachable and means something.

**Desk Rating** converts your best result per game to a z, averages, and shrinks toward the
median by `n/(n+3)`. Playing three games well cannot make you top 1% of the desk.

**One correction applied on top of the raw model:** `N` comes out as an integer number of
items, so two adjacent bands sometimes land on the same `N` and collapse to nearly the same
score — a 10-point gap between p90 and p95 is meaningless next to a run standard deviation of
400. Where that happened, p95 was pushed out to at least **30% of the way from p90 to p99**
(its z-value sits at 35% of that interval, so this is a conservative floor rather than a
reposition). Thirteen of the 48 anchors were respaced this way; no p50, p90 or p99 was
touched.

Target Sum has no `sep` figure because wrong answers are impossible there — the model's
only free parameter is throughput, so simulated runs at a fixed speed are identical. Real
run-to-run variance comes from how solvable the generated grids happen to be.

### Recalibration of corrected games — 2026-09-17

Games 08 and 10 were recalibrated after the verification fixes changed their
scoring paths. The original scratch calibrator was not present in repository
history, so these values are a transparent reconstruction rather than a claim of
bit-for-bit reproduction: `verification/python/calibration/pnl_recalibration.py`
runs 4,000 simulations for each of four explicitly named policies per variant.
The p50/p90/p95/p99 anchors are the 50th/90th/95th/99th percentile of the
corresponding policy distribution; `sd` is the standard deviation of the p90
policy, and `sep = (p90 − p50) / sd`. Inventory Skew uses the corrected full
liquidation path. Toxic Flow uses the corrected single realized-PnL award.

The policy runs use the shipped 80-second windows and variant mechanics. Inventory
Skew policies are no skew, then position-control gains 0.4/0.8/1.2 with 1.6/1.2/0.8
second reactions. Toxic Flow policies are fixed acceptance thresholds 0.35/0.25/0.15
followed by the per-counterparty Bayesian posterior policy. The resulting anchors
are monotonic and are now copied into the corresponding `th:` blocks in the game
files.

### Balance changes the simulation forced

Building this table exposed four games that were scoring the wrong thing. All four were
changed, and the anchors above are for the fixed versions:

- **08 Inventory Skew** — carrying inventory was a martingale, so risk control had zero
  expected value and the "never skew" policy outscored every disciplined one. A **capital
  charge proportional to position²**, applied every second, now makes flatness pay. Skew
  cost was also reduced (tick 0.05 → 0.02) and volatility raised so the trade-off is real.
- **10 Toxic Flow** — average edge exceeded average toxicity cost, so accepting
  indiscriminately was profitable and learning bought nothing. Edges were tightened and
  toxicity made **bimodal** (roughly half the names near-clean, half near-always-informed),
  which also makes the learning problem solvable inside 80 seconds.
- **12 Winner's Curse** — with a perfectly competitive field, *no* bid is profitable, which
  is the correct theory and a terrible game: every policy scored ≈ 0. The field is now
  explicitly **cautious** (rivals over-shade 1.2–3.2σ), which leaves money on the table for
  a disciplined bidder while a naive bidder still gets cursed.
- **24 Kelly Sizing** — the score floor at zero was rewarding overbetting, because
  catastrophes were truncated. Fixed by starting you with a **book value of 1,250 points to
  lose**, staking each decision over a **block of 6 bets** so compounding is visible rather
  than lucky, and scoring the **sizing decision itself** alongside realised growth.
- **07 Quote the Market** — scoring the realised settlement meant one draw of V had a larger
  standard deviation than the entire edge; skill was invisible. You are now marked at **fair
  value** with adverse selection charged at its **expected** cost, and each quote faces five
  counterparties. Separation went from 0.28 to 5.10.

## 6. Which games can actually judge you in one run

`sep` from the tables above, interpreted:

- **Reliable in a single run** (sep > 2): every mental-maths, probability and detection game.
- **Usable but noisy** (sep 1.0–2.0): Inventory Skew.
- **Needs several runs** (sep < 1.0): Toxic Flow, Winner's Curse, and especially **Kelly
  Sizing standard (0.29)**. These are variance machines by nature — a common-value auction
  where you win 30% of the time cannot be judged on nine auctions. Take a median of five runs
  before believing the band.

## 7. Known limitations

- **The model is the weak link, not the maths.** The interpolation, the normal CDF and the
  Monte-Carlo are all exact. The `(timeFactor, accuracy)` table in §3 is an informed guess.
  If you think the median applicant is quicker than modelled, every band shifts up and your
  percentile shifts down.
- **Only one published benchmark anchors anything.** Optiver's 80-questions-in-8-minutes
  test has a widely-reported pass mark near 60% correct, which is roughly the p50 row for
  game 13. Everything else is reasoned from response-time and error-rate ranges for the
  underlying cognitive task, not from quant-specific data.
- **No learning-curve adjustment.** Your fifth run at a game benefits from knowing its
  generators. Real assessments are first-exposure, so treat early runs as the honest ones.
- **Trading games reward one specific model of the world.** Toxic Flow assumes fixed
  per-counterparty toxicity; a real desk faces adaptive counterparties. The skill transfers;
  the exact optimum does not.
